# Pastiche — reference

Read this when `adapting-existing-work` identifies the intended relationship as pastiche (homage). It mirrors PSALM's Pastiche evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.4.2), grounded in Article 5(3)(k) of the InfoSoc Directive and, more specifically, Advocate General Emiliou's Opinion in *Pelham II* (Case C-590/23), which is the fullest recent treatment of what distinguishes pastiche from parody and caricature under EU law.

## What Separates Pastiche from Parody

Both exceptions permit borrowing from an existing work, and both can involve strong stylistic evocation of the source. The dividing line AG Emiliou draws is **character of the relationship to the source**: parody requires a noticeable difference and is typically understood as at least implicitly commenting on or transforming the source (see `parody-and-satire.md`); pastiche is centrally about **imitation of style as a form of artistic tribute** — it can be affectionate, playful, or reverential without needing to critique or mock anything, and without needing to differ substantively in content from the source's world or characters the way parody does.

Practically: if the intended piece imitates a source's voice and technique as tribute, with no critical or mocking intent, pastiche is the better-fitting exception to work through; if it's evoking the source specifically to comment on or subvert it, use `parody-and-satire.md` instead.

## The Five Sub-Dimensions

### Style Evocation and Recognition

Which source-specific stylistic signatures are being imitated — sentence rhythm, lexical choices, tonal qualities, characteristic narrative techniques (see `story-brainstorming/references/writing-style.md` for the full sub-dimension vocabulary to draw on here). A high answer means the imitation is source-specific, not just genre-typical ("writes like Dickens" is a specific claim; "writes in old-fashioned English" is not).

### Artistic Skill and Execution

How the imitation stays coherent and adapts naturally to new content, rather than reading as clumsy or inconsistent mimicry. AG Emiliou's Opinion frames pastiche as requiring genuine "artistic creation," not a mechanical pastiche of surface tics — this sub-dimension is the check for whether the imitation is actually skilled.

### Homage / Tribute Character

Evidence of respect or admiration for the source, and — this is the sub-dimension that most directly distinguishes pastiche from parody — the *absence* of mockery or critique. Tone, framing, and any dedicatory gestures are the relevant signals.

### Noticeable Differences / Transformation

Whether the content (characters, plot, themes) substantially differs from the source despite the stylistic imitation — pastiche still requires *some* distance from the source even while embracing its style closely.

### Fair Balance and Proportionality

The extent of stylistic borrowing relative to how much new content the piece contributes, and whether the result could plausibly substitute for the source in the market. Doctrinal commentary (Rendas 2024) has raised concerns about pastiche being used as a "catch-all" exception for extensive borrowing that wouldn't survive scrutiny under a narrower doctrine — this sub-dimension is the check against that misuse.

## Further Reading

- Court of Justice of the European Union, Advocate General Emiliou's Opinion, *Pelham II* (Case C-590/23, 2025) — the primary source for the artistic-creation and homage-character criteria above.
- European Parliament and Council, Directive 2001/29/EC (InfoSoc), Art. 5(3)(k) — the statutory basis for the pastiche exception.
- Rendas, T. (2024), "The pastiche exception after *Pelham II*," *European Intellectual Property Review* 46(12) — the fair-balance and proportionality concerns above draw on this discussion.
