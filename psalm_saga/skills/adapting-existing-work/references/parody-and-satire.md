# Parody and Satire — reference

Read this when `adapting-existing-work` identifies the intended relationship as parody or satire. It mirrors PSALM's Parody and Satire evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.4.1), which operationalises the criteria the Court of Justice of the European Union set out in *Deckmyn* (Case C-201/13, 2014), as subsequently interpreted by doctrinal commentary (Rendas 2024 on the pastiche exception's boundaries after *Pelham II*, discussing parody's adjacent doctrine).

Reminder from `adapting-existing-work`'s own text: nothing here is legal advice or a determination that a given story would qualify for the exception. It's a structured way to make sure the creative choices that *Deckmyn* treats as relevant are made deliberately rather than left to chance.

## The Deckmyn Criteria, in Practice

*Deckmyn* identified two core requirements for something to count as parody under EU law: it must **evoke an existing work** while being **noticeably different from it**, and — the Court added — its application is subject to a **fair balance** between the interests of the rightsholder and the freedom of expression of the parodist, which can be lost if the parody conveys a discriminatory message.

The PSALM evaluator breaks this into five sub-dimensions:

### Source Work Evocation / Recognition

How clearly the story evokes the specific source — named references, distinctive characters, iconic settings, or recognisable stylistic imitation (see `writing-style.md` and `narrative-voice.md` in `story-brainstorming/references/` for what "stylistic imitation" concretely means). Weak, generic evocation ("a story about wizards") isn't enough to trigger parody analysis at all — the reader has to actually be able to identify *this specific source*.

### Noticeable Differences / Transformation

Substantive changes to characters, plot, setting, or message — not merely cosmetic substitutions (renaming a character while keeping everything else identical does not satisfy this). *Deckmyn*'s own facts involved substituting the political message of a comic-book cover while keeping its visual style recognisable; the transformation has to be doing real work, not just providing legal cover.

### Humorous Character

The devices carrying the humour: exaggeration, irony, absurdity, wordplay. *Deckmyn* does not require any particular comedic form or intensity, but PSALM's evaluator (and this skill) still asks you to name the actual devices rather than asserting "it's funny" as a bare conclusion.

### Mocking / Critical Character

What is being critiqued, and how: the source work itself, its author, a genre, or some broader social phenomenon the source is being used as a vehicle to comment on. Parody can be primarily critical without being primarily comedic — the two sub-dimensions above and this one are independent axes, not restatements of each other.

### Fair Balance and Context

Is the extent of borrowing proportionate to the parodic purpose? And, following *Deckmyn*'s own explicit warning: does the work risk conveying a discriminatory message against a protected group? This is the one sub-dimension in this evaluator that functions as something closer to a hard stop than a matter of degree — if the honest answer raises a discrimination concern, that's a reason to reconsider the approach, not a style note to soften.

## Further Reading

- Rendas, T. (2024), "The pastiche exception after *Pelham II*: Implications for generative AI," *European Intellectual Property Review* 46(12) — useful for understanding how parody's neighbouring exceptions (pastiche, quotation) are drawn and where the boundaries between them sit; see `pastiche.md` for the adjacent doctrine.
