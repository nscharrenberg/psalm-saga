# Scènes à Faire — reference

Read this for **every** adaptation regardless of which relationship (`parody-and-satire.md`, `pastiche.md`, or `quotation-and-citation.md`) applies — unlike those three, this sub-dimension set is always relevant when working from an existing source. It mirrors PSALM's Scènes à Faire evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.4.4), grounded in the idea-expression dichotomy and, specifically, the line of U.S. case law that gives the doctrine its name and its clearest articulation: *Nichols v. Universal Pictures Corp.* (1930), *Hoehling v. Universal City Studios* (1980), *Walker v. Time Life Films* (1986), and *Warner Bros. Entertainment v. RDR Books* (2008, the "Harry Potter Lexicon" case).

## The Core Idea

*Scènes à faire* ("scenes that must be done") are elements so standard to a genre, setting, or subject matter that they're effectively required by the choice of that genre or setting, rather than being the author's own creative contribution. A detective story requires *some* investigative process; a medieval-fantasy story requires *some* version of castles, kingdoms, and swordplay. Copyright doesn't protect the genre furniture itself, only what a particular author does with it beyond the generic requirement.

This matters directly for adaptation: an adapted story that leans heavily on genre-standard elements shared with its source is on much safer ground than one that reproduces the source's *specific, non-generic* creative choices — and, separately, leaning on genre-standard elements from the source doesn't make the new story distinctive in its own right either. This reference exists to help separate the two.

## The Six Sub-Dimensions

### Genre Conventions and Setting

Genre-specific plot structures and standard settings — a crime leading to investigation and revelation, a medieval-fantasy kingdom with castles. Identify which elements of the source (and the new story) are simply "what this genre does."

### Stock Characters and Archetypes

Whether key characters fit archetypal moulds without further elaboration — the reluctant hero, the wise mentor. See `story-brainstorming/references/character.md`'s treatment of Character Function and Role, which names exactly this same weakly-protectable territory from the generative side.

### Standard Plot Devices and Tropes

Common devices such as love triangles, mistaken identity, or a last-minute rescue — recognisable structural patterns available across many works in a tradition, not unique to the source.

### Thematic Commonplaces

Generic treatment of broad themes (good versus evil, coming of age). Themes as such are unprotectable ideas regardless of genericness — the *treatment* of a theme is where originality can live, if it's distinctive enough.

### Necessary Technical Elements

Elements required by genre or medium mechanics — basic investigative steps in a detective story, standard scene-transition conventions, expository setup a story of this kind more or less has to do somehow.

### Creative Elaboration

The inverse of the five sub-dimensions above: the degree of distinctive, original expression present across style, characterisation, world-building, and structure, beyond what genre requires. This is the corrective term — a high score on genre-typical elements combined with genuinely low creative elaboration is the combination worth being honest about.

**Feed the answer to this sub-dimension back into the core spec.** Naming which elements are borrowed and generic is only half the exercise; the other half is making sure the new story's *own* elaboration on top of that shared foundation is specific enough to matter — which is exactly what `story-brainstorming`'s core six dimensions (see `story-brainstorming/references/`) are for. If Creative Elaboration comes out thin, that's a signal to go back and sharpen the core dimension answers, not just a note to file away.

## Further Reading

- *Nichols v. Universal Pictures Corp.*, 45 F.2d 119 (2d Cir. 1930) — the foundational case articulating the idea-expression line this doctrine sits on.
- *Hoehling v. Universal City Studios*, 618 F.2d 972 (2d Cir. 1980) — historical-event-based works and the limits of protecting standard treatments of real events.
- *Walker v. Time Life Films*, 784 F.2d 44 (2d Cir. 1986) — genre-standard elements in crime fiction specifically.
- *Warner Bros. Entertainment v. RDR Books*, 575 F. Supp. 2d 513 (S.D.N.Y. 2008) — application of the doctrine to a richly detailed fantasy world (the *Harry Potter* "Lexicon" case), useful background if the adaptation involves an elaborate secondary world.
