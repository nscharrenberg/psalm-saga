# Scene Sequence — reference

Read this when working through the Scene Sequence dimension in `story-brainstorming`. It mirrors PSALM's Scene Sequence Similarity evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.3.5), grounded in film-narrative theory (Bordwell and Thompson 2012; Dancyger 2010) and screenwriting craft (McKee 1997) — disciplines that pay unusually close attention to the scene, rather than the whole plot or a single sentence, as the working unit of narrative construction.

Scene Sequence sits at a smaller grain than Plot Structure: Plot Structure asks about the causal chain of *events*; Scene Sequence asks about how those events are *staged and paced* as discrete, orderable units the reader experiences one at a time. A single plot event might span several scenes, or several plot beats might be compressed into one scene — deciding how is exactly what this dimension is for.

## Scene Identity and Content

Setting, participants, actions, and narrative purpose for each major scene — enough concrete specificity that a reader would recognise a particular scene as *that* scene rather than a generic instance of its type ("the confrontation scene," "the reveal scene").

- This is the sub-dimension most sensitive to the difference between a distinctive scene and a stock one — see `adapting-existing-work/references/scenes-a-faire.md` if this story is an adaptation, since genre-standard scene types (the training montage, the villain's monologue) are exactly what that doctrine treats as weakly protectable.

## Scene Internal Structure

The beat sequence and staging *within* a single scene — how it opens, builds, and closes; where the turn or reveal within the scene itself lands.

- McKee's (1997) treatment of scene-level beats (a scene as itself a miniature structure with its own turning point, not just a container for dialogue) is the direct grounding here. A scene without an internal turn tends to read as flat regardless of how important its content is to the larger plot.

## Scene Sequence Architecture

The ordering of scenes and the relationships between them — which scene follows which, and why that order rather than another.

- Distinct from Plot Structure's Story Architecture (see `plot-structure.md`), which concerns acts, framing devices, and subplot interweaving at a larger grain; Scene Sequence Architecture is specifically about the scene-to-scene ordering decisions within that larger shape.

## Scene Transition and Connections

The linking devices between scenes and how continuity is maintained or deliberately broken — a hard cut, a match cut on an image or line of dialogue, a slow dissolve via a shared detail, an explicit time-jump marker.

- Distinct from Writing Style's Discourse Organisation (see `writing-style.md`), which concerns paragraph-to-paragraph connective tissue *within* a scene. Transitions here are specifically about the seams between scenes.

## Scene Pacing and Rhythm

Patterns of scene length and tempo across the work — does the story favour many short scenes in quick succession, a few long immersive ones, or an alternating rhythm between the two (often used to vary tension)?

## Scene Functions and Types

The standard categories of scene used and where they fall (the inciting-incident scene, the midpoint reversal, the climax, the denouement).

- Like Character Function and Role and Plot Functions and Convergence, this is the most genre-typical, least distinctive sub-dimension in this group — most narrative traditions rely on broadly similar scene-type vocabularies. A brief answer naming which standard scene types this story uses and roughly where is sufficient; the real distinctiveness belongs in Scene Identity and Content and Scene Internal Structure above.
