# Writing Style — reference

Read this when working through the Writing Style dimension in `story-brainstorming`. It expands each sub-dimension with a working definition, what to actually listen for, and a pitfall to avoid. It mirrors PSALM's Writing Style evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.3.1), which is itself grounded in authorship-attribution and stylometric research: the idea that an author's word choice and sentence construction function as something close to a signature, largely independent of what the text is *about* (Stamatatos 2009's survey of authorship-attribution methods; Juola 2008's foundational treatment of the same field; Hoover 1973 on prose rhythm as a matter of proportional distribution across a text, not a single trait).

Keep content and style separate while working this dimension: two texts can describe entirely different events in the same style, or the same event in wholly different styles. This dimension asks about the *how*, not the *what*.

## Lexical Complexity

Vocabulary richness (how repetitive vs. varied the word choices are), typical word length, and formality register (colloquial contractions and simple diction vs. elevated, Latinate vocabulary).

- Ask for a committed answer, not a spectrum: "plain, mostly one- and two-syllable words, minimal adjectives" reads very differently from "ornate, multisyllabic, adjective-heavy."
- Formality register interacts with Tone & Voice below but is a distinct, more mechanical choice — it's about the words themselves, not the narrator's attitude.
- Pitfall: "rich vocabulary" is not an answer. Rich compared to what, and in which direction — more varied synonyms, or longer/rarer individual words? These pull in different directions.

## Sentence Structure

Typical sentence length and its distribution (not just an average — does the text lean toward short declaratives with occasional long ones for emphasis, or the reverse?), and syntactic complexity (subordination depth, parallel constructions, sentence fragments used deliberately).

- Hoover (1973) frames prose rhythm as a matter of *proportional distribution* across a whole text rather than a single typical sentence — so a useful answer names both a baseline and where it deliberately breaks (e.g. "mostly short, but action climaxes get one long cumulative sentence").
- Pitfall: "varied sentence length" without a distribution shape is not committing to anything a reviewer or a later PSALM run could actually check against.

## Rhythm & Flow

Punctuation density (heavy use of commas, dashes, and semicolons vs. sparse, mostly full stops), pacing, and the felt rhythm of reading the prose aloud.

- This sub-dimension is where discourse-marker choices (Biber and Finegan's 1988 work on stance markers and connective devices) show up at the sentence level — words like "however," "still," "of course" that modulate flow and authorial stance simultaneously.
- Pitfall: rhythm is easy to leave implicit ("it should just flow well"). Name at least one concrete device — short staccato sentences at tense moments, long unbroken sentences for a dreamlike quality, heavy use of em-dashes for interruption.

## Rhetorical Patterns

Use of rhetorical questions, imperatives, repetition (anaphora — repeating an opening phrase; epistrophe — repeating a closing phrase), and parallelism.

- Herrmann, van Dalen-Oskam, and Schöch (2015) treat these patterned repetitions as part of what makes "style" a durable, identifiable construct rather than a vague impression — they're countable, specific choices.
- Pitfall: don't default to "none, this is plain prose." Plainness is itself a rhetorical choice (the deliberate *absence* of these devices) and worth stating as such if that's the intent.

## Discourse Organisation

Paragraph structure (long, multi-sentence paragraphs with explicit connective markers, or short, stand-alone paragraphs that let white space do the work) and how transitions between ideas are signalled.

- Pitfall: this is easy to conflate with Scene Sequence's transition sub-dimension (see the Scene Sequence reference). Discourse Organisation is about paragraph-to-paragraph and sentence-to-sentence connective tissue *within* a scene; Scene Sequence's transitions are about scene-to-scene jumps. Keep the two separate when writing the spec so a reviewer can tell which one a given passage is answering.

## Tone & Voice

Personal vs. impersonal narratorial stance, and assertive vs. hedging phrasing (does the prose state things flatly, or qualify and soften them?).

- This is the sub-dimension most likely to get confused with the *Narrative Voice* dimension (see `references/narrative-voice.md`) — they share the word "voice" but ask different questions. Writing Style's Tone & Voice is about word-and-phrase-level attitude (assertive vs. hedging, warm vs. clinical diction); Narrative Voice is about structural point-of-view choices (who is telling the story and from where). A text can have a warm, assertive tone (Writing Style) delivered through a distant, omniscient third-person narrator (Narrative Voice) — these are independent choices, not the same question asked twice.
- Booth's (1961) account of the implied author is useful background here: tone is one of the main levers through which a narrator's implied attitude toward the material comes through, independent of what actually happens in the story.
