# World-Building — reference

Read this when working through the World-Building dimension in `story-brainstorming`. It mirrors PSALM's World Building Similarity evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.3.6), grounded in fantasy/genre scholarship on how fictional worlds are constructed as coherent systems (Wolf 2012's account of "subcreation" — building an imaginary world with its own internal logic; Ekman 2013 on how fantasy maps and settings encode history and geography together) and in text-world theory (Gavins 2007), which treats the sensory and experiential texture of a world as something readers actively construct from cues in the text, not something simply described at them.

This dimension matters even for stories with a contemporary, "real-world" setting — every story world has geography, rules (even if the rule is "this is our world, physics as usual"), culture, history, and sensory texture, whether or not any of it is exotic. Answer every sub-dimension even for a mundane setting; "unremarkable, present-day, no departures from consensus reality" is itself a specific, useful answer.

## Geographic and Spatial Design

Locations, spatial layout, and environmental features, specific enough that a reader could roughly map the world in their head — not just named places, but how they relate to each other spatially.

- A distinctive city layout or landscape structure is one of the more concrete, checkable forms of world-building originality (the paper treats copying a specific map or spatial layout as a stronger similarity signal than sharing a generic setting type, e.g. "a fantasy kingdom").

## World Rules and System

The magic, technology, or physics system governing the world: specific rules, costs, capabilities, and limitations.

- Wolf's (2012) treatment of world-building as subcreation is directly about this sub-dimension: a fantasy or science-fiction system becomes distinctive precisely through its specific costs and limits, not through the mere presence of magic or advanced technology as a category. "Magic exists" is not a system; "magic requires a physical sacrifice proportional to the effect, and using it on another person without consent visibly marks the caster" is.

## Cultural and Social Architecture

Customs, rituals, institutions, and social hierarchies specific to this world or community.

## Historical and Temporal Design

Past events, historical cycles, and eras that inform the present-day world of the story.

- Ekman's (2013) work on fantasy maps ties geography and history together deliberately — a distinctive world's history often explains *why* its geography and institutions look the way they do, rather than the two being independently arbitrary.

## Material and Sensory Detail

Objects, flora and fauna, and the sensory atmosphere of the world — the concrete, textural details a reader experiences rather than is told about.

- Gavins' (2007) text-world theory is the direct grounding here: readers actively construct an experiential sense of a fictional world from accumulated sensory cues, so distinctive material detail (a specific smell, a recurring object, an unusual plant) does real work in making a world feel authored rather than generic, even in small doses.

## World Function and Logic

How the world stays internally coherent and operates as a system — the most abstract sub-dimension, closer to a functional property ("the world makes sense") than to a specific creative choice.

- This is the lowest-weighted sub-dimension in PSALM's own aggregation for exactly this reason: internal consistency is closer to a baseline craft requirement than to distinctive expression. A brief confirmation that the rules established above are mutually consistent is enough; don't over-invest here relative to the more concrete sub-dimensions above.
