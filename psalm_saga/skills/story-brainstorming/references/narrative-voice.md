# Narrative Voice — reference

Read this when working through the Narrative Voice dimension in `story-brainstorming`. It mirrors PSALM's Narrative Voice evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.3.2), grounded almost entirely in classical narratology — chiefly Genette's (1980) taxonomy of narrative person, distance, and focalisation, and Booth's (1961) and Phelan's (2005) work on narrative distance and the rhetoric of character narration.

Narrative Voice is about the *structural* position from which the story is told — who narrates, from where, how close to the characters, and in what order relative to the events. It's a smaller, more tightly specified set of choices than Writing Style, and getting them internally consistent matters more than being elaborate about any one of them.

## Point of View

First, second, or third person, and — for third person specifically — the narrator's knowledge scope: limited (confined to one character's awareness), multiple (shifts between a few characters' awareness, usually per scene or chapter), omniscient (unrestricted access to any character's thoughts), or objective (no access to any character's interiority at all, purely external observation).

- Genette's (1980) framework treats person and knowledge scope as logically separate choices — third-person doesn't imply omniscient, and first-person doesn't preclude a narrator who withholds their own thoughts strategically. State both explicitly.
- Pitfall: "third person" alone is an incomplete answer. Third-person limited and third-person omniscient produce very different stories even with an identical plot.

## Narrative Distance

How close the narrator sits to a character's consciousness (do we get their exact thoughts in something close to their own words, a paraphrased summary of their mental state, or only their outward behaviour with no interiority at all?) and how emotionally involved the narrating voice seems to be.

- Booth (1961) and Phelan (2005) treat distance as a spectrum the narration can move along *within* a single text, not just a fixed setting — a story might sit close to a character for interior moments and pull back to a more panoramic distance for action or transition. If the story deliberately shifts distance, say so and say when.
- Pitfall: don't conflate distance with Point of View's knowledge scope. A limited third-person narrator can sit very close to a character (near-interior-monologue) or quite far (reporting only actions and dialogue) — distance is a separate dial from how much the narrator is *allowed* to know.

## Narrator Presence

Whether the narrator is homodiegetic (a participant in the story's events, even if not the protagonist) or heterodiegetic (entirely outside the story world), and how intrusive or self-commenting the narrating voice is (does it ever address the reader directly, editorialise, or acknowledge itself as a telling rather than a transparent window onto events?).

- Stanzel (1984) and Fludernik (1996) are the standard references for narrator-presence typologies beyond Genette's basic split; the practical question for the spec is simpler: can the narrator ever say "I," referring to itself as a figure in or around the events, and does it ever step outside strict scene-reporting to comment?
- Pitfall: an unnamed first-person narrator who never appears as a character (e.g. narrating events they only heard about secondhand) is still homodiegetic if they exist within the story world at all — homodiegetic doesn't require being a major character.

## Temporal Perspective

The primary verb tense, and the narrator's temporal position relative to the events: retrospective (telling about the past, possibly with hindsight the characters didn't have at the time), simultaneous (narrating events as if they're unfolding now), or anticipatory (narrating with knowledge of, or foreshadowing toward, events that haven't happened in the telling yet).

- This is a distinct question from Plot Structure's Temporal Structure sub-dimension (see `references/plot-structure.md`), which is about chronological ordering of *events* (flashbacks, non-linear plotting). Temporal Perspective here is about the narrator's own vantage point in time relative to what's being told, independent of how the events themselves are sequenced on the page.

## Focalisation Pattern

Whose perspective filters the information the reader receives — fixed internal (always through one character), variable internal (rotating between a few characters, typically per chapter or section), or external (no privileged access to anyone's interior, camera-like).

- Genette's (1980) concept of focalisation is deliberately kept separate from grammatical person: a third-person narration can still be tightly focalised through one character (we only ever know what they know, even though it's told "about" them rather than "by" them). Confirm this distinction is clear in the spec's answer rather than treating focalisation as a restatement of Point of View.

## Reader Engagement

Whether and how the narrator addresses the reader directly (second-person asides, rhetorical "you," explicit acknowledgement of the act of telling), how often, and with what assumed relationship (intimate confidant, neutral reporter, formal address).

- This is the lowest-weighted sub-dimension in PSALM's own aggregation — it's often governed more by genre and tradition (children's literature and some comic narration address the reader far more than literary fiction typically does) than by individual authorial signature. A brief, decisive answer is fine here; don't over-invest relative to Point of View and Narrative Distance.
