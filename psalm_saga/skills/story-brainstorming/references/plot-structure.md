# Plot Structure — reference

Read this when working through the Plot Structure dimension in `story-brainstorming`. It mirrors PSALM's Plot Structure Similarity evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.3.4), grounded in dramaturgical and screenwriting theory on how events causally chain together into a structure (chiefly McKee 1997's treatment of story substance and structure) and in narratological work on story time and organisation (Genette 1980; Bordwell and Thompson's 2012 film-theory account of large-scale narrative architecture, which the paper also draws on for the adjacent Scene Sequence dimension).

The throughline across every sub-dimension here: plot is a *causal chain*, not a list of things that happen. "And then" is a sequence; "and therefore" or "but" is a plot (a formulation close to McKee's own emphasis on causality as what separates a story from a mere chronicle of events).

## Event Sequence and Causality

The specific chain of plot events and, critically, the causal links between them — what happens *because of* what.

- Answer this as a chain with explicit causal connectors, not a bullet list of events. "X happens, which forces Y to do Z, which reveals W" is testable; "X happens, then Y happens, then Z happens" describes a sequence but not yet a plot.

## Story Architecture and Structure

The large-scale organisation: act divisions, framing devices (a story told within a story), nested narratives, and how subplots interweave with the main plot.

- Bordwell and Thompson's (2012) account of large-scale narrative form and Dancyger's (2010) treatment of structural editing patterns are useful background for naming a shape precisely — "three-act with a frame narrative bookending it," "braided dual-timeline," "single continuous scene in real time" are all specific architectures; "a beginning, middle, and end" is not specific enough to plan or review against.

## Conflict Construction

The escalation pattern and the network of obstacles the protagonist (or protagonists) face — how difficulty compounds over the course of the story rather than staying flat.

- McKee's (1997) treatment of rising action and complication is the direct theoretical source here: conflict construction is about the *pattern* of escalation (steady build, plateau-then-spike, multiple simultaneous fronts) as much as any individual obstacle.

## Plot Turning Points and Reversals

The pivotal shifts and revelations — specifically the *unique* twists particular to this story, not generic "and then something surprising happened."

- This sub-dimension is where courts and commentators have historically focused close attention in plot-similarity disputes (the paper cites Hoehling v. Universal City Studios and related U.S. case law as illustrative of this focus, in the context of *adapting existing work* — see `adapting-existing-work/references/scenes-a-faire.md` for that angle): the specific shape of a twist is far more distinctive, and far more worth deciding deliberately, than the generic fact that a story contains one.

## Temporal Structure

Chronology, flashbacks, and the pacing of time within the plot — a distinct question from Narrative Voice's Temporal Perspective (see `narrative-voice.md`), which concerns the narrator's own vantage point in time rather than how the events themselves are ordered on the page.

- Genette's (1980) treatment of narrative order (the gap between story-order — what actually happened when — and discourse-order — the order the reader receives it in) is the relevant theory here. Decide explicitly whether this story's discourse-order matches its story-order, and if not, exactly where and why it diverges (a single extended flashback vs. a scrambled, achronological structure are very different commitments).

## Plot Functions and Convergence

How subplot threads converge and resolve relative to each other and to the main plot.

- Like Character Function and Role, and Scene Functions and Types, this sub-dimension tends toward genre-typical, weakly distinctive territory (subplots converging at a climax is close to a structural default across most narrative traditions). A brief, clear answer is fine; the real distinctiveness usually lives in Event Sequence and Causality and in the specific Turning Points above.
