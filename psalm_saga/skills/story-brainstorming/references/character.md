# Character — reference

Read this when working through the Character dimension in `story-brainstorming`. It mirrors PSALM's Character Similarity evaluator (Scharrenberg & Sun, §3.3.3), grounded in cognitive-narratological work on how readers construct a sense of character from a text (chiefly Palmer 2004's account of "fictional minds"), and in legal/critical commentary that distinguishes generic character archetypes (weakly protectable, easily reused) from specific, elaborated characterisation (the more distinctive and legally significant end of the spectrum).

The organising idea across every sub-dimension below: specificity is what makes a character original rather than a type. "A grieving widow" is an archetype available to anyone; the exact texture of *how* a specific character grieves — what they do with their hands, what they refuse to talk about, who they snap at — is what makes a character distinctive. Push every answer toward specific, concrete detail rather than a category label.

## Character Identity and Traits

Distinctive personality traits, physical idiosyncrasies, and mannerisms — the concrete, repeatable details a reader would recognise the character by.

- Palmer's (2004) account of readers building a "continuing consciousness" for a character out of accumulated textual cues is the theoretical backing here: identity isn't stated once, it's built from a pattern of small, consistent details across the text.
- Pitfall: "kind but stubborn" is a trait *label*, not a trait *rendered*. What does stubbornness look like in this specific character — do they repeat the same sentence three times instead of raising their voice? Refuse to ask for help even when it costs them? Name the behaviour, not just the adjective.

## Character Arc and Development

The character's initial state, the trigger that sets change in motion, the stages of that change, and the resolution.

- Structure this as an actual sequence when answering, not just an end state — "starts guarded, a betrayal early on makes them more guarded still, a specific act of trust from someone else cracks that, they end willing to ask for help for the first time" is a testable arc; "grows as a person" is not.

## Character Relationships and Dynamics

Interaction patterns between characters, power balances, and the emotional texture of specific relationships (not just "they're friends" but what kind of friction and warmth that friendship actually contains).

- Palmer's (2004) broader argument that fictional minds are constructed *relationally*, through interaction with other minds in the text, applies directly here: a character is defined partly by how they specifically behave with each other character, not only by traits listed in isolation.

## Character Background and Motivation

Specific causal history and motivational structure — not a generic backstory label ("traumatic past," "poor upbringing") but the actual events and the causal chain from those events to present behaviour.

- The paper treats this sub-dimension as one of the places originality most often actually resides, precisely because it's easy to leave generic and easy to make specific: two characters can share the label "orphan" while having entirely distinct, non-interchangeable histories that produce different present-day behaviour.

## Character Expression and Behaviour

Behavioural signatures and habitual emotional responses — how this character specifically expresses anger, fear, affection, or humour, as a repeated pattern rather than a one-off description.

## Character Function and Role

The character's high-level narrative function (protagonist, antagonist, mentor, foil).

- This is the weakest, most genre-typical sub-dimension — narrative roles like "mentor" or "antagonist" are close to unprotectable *scènes à faire* territory (see `adapting-existing-work/references/scenes-a-faire.md` if this story is an adaptation) precisely because nearly every story has one. Answer it briefly and put the real creative weight into the sub-dimensions above.
